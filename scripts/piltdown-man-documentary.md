## ACT ONE: A GRAVEL PIT IN SUSSEX (0:45 – 2:30)

**[ON SCREEN: Drone footage of the green fields of East Sussex. A map graphic marks Piltdown, near Uckfield.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Our story begins in the English countryside, with a man named Charles Dawson.

**[ON SCREEN: Portrait of Charles Dawson: moustache, bowler hat, a confident gaze.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Dawson was a country solicitor, but his real passion was the past. He was an amateur collector of fossils, flints and antiquities, and he was a very lucky one. He found so many remarkable things that friends called him "the wizard of Sussex."

He was also hungry for the one thing an amateur could rarely get: recognition from the scientific establishment.

**[ON SCREEN: An archival letter, handwriting animated across the screen.]**

**NARRATOR:**
In February 1912, Dawson wrote to Arthur Smith Woodward, Keeper of Geology at the British Museum of Natural History and one of the most respected fossil experts in the world.

Dawson said that, a few years earlier, workmen digging gravel at Barkham Manor near Piltdown had given him a piece of a thick human skull. He had been searching the pit ever since, and he had found more.

**[ON SCREEN: Reconstruction of men sieving gravel by a pond. A young man in a clerical collar works alongside them.]**

**NARRATOR:**
That summer, Woodward joined him in the pit, along with a young French Jesuit priest and geology student, Pierre Teilhard de Chardin.

They found more pieces of the skull, primitive-looking flint tools and the fossil teeth of ancient animals: elephant, mastodon and hippopotamus.

Then they found the most important piece of all: half of a lower jaw, with two worn molars still in place.

**[MUSIC: Builds.]**

## ACT TWO: THE DAWN MAN (2:30 – 4:00)

**[ON SCREEN: Exterior of Burlington House, London. Gaslight. A crowded lecture hall.]**

**NARRATOR:**
On the 18th of December 1912, Dawson and Woodward presented their discovery to the Geological Society of London.

The skull, they said, was large and rounded, almost human. The jaw was primitive and apelike. Together they made a creature that was half man and half ape: the long-sought "missing link."

Woodward gave it a scientific name, *Eoanthropus dawsoni*, "Dawson's dawn man."

**[ON SCREEN: A map of Europe. Pins appear at the Neander Valley in Germany, Heidelberg, and Java.]**

**NARRATOR:**
To understand why this mattered so much, you have to understand the time.

Germany had its Neanderthals and its Heidelberg jaw. France had its cave art and its Cro-Magnons. Britain, the heart of an empire and the home of Charles Darwin, had almost nothing.

Now, suddenly, it had the oldest human in Europe.

**[ON SCREEN: A 1913 newspaper headline: "THE EARLIEST MAN?"]**

**NARRATOR:**
The find also fit a popular idea of the time: that humans had become human brain-first. Our big brains came early, and our bodies caught up later. Piltdown seemed to prove it.

When a scientific discovery tells people what they already believe, they tend not to ask many questions.

**[ON SCREEN: A sketch of a canine tooth, then a carved bone shaped like a cricket bat.]**

**NARRATOR:**
And the finds kept coming. In 1913, Teilhard picked up an apelike canine tooth, exactly the kind the experts had predicted. In 1914 came a strange tool carved from an elephant's thigh bone. Its shape earned it a nickname: the "cricket bat."

In 1915, Dawson reported a second set of remains from another site a few miles away. Piltdown II.

One find could be luck. Two finds looked like proof.

## ACT THREE: THE DOUBTERS (4:00 – 5:30)

**[MUSIC: Becomes uneasy.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Not everyone was convinced.

**[ON SCREEN: Portraits appear one at a time, each with a name and date caption.]**

**NARRATOR:**
In 1913, David Waterston, an anatomist at King's College London, argued that the skull and jaw simply did not belong together. The skull was human. The jaw looked like an ape's.

In 1915, Gerrit Miller of the Smithsonian Institution came to the same conclusion: the jaw was from a chimpanzee. Later, the German anatomist Franz Weidenreich suggested it belonged to an orangutan, with teeth that had been filed down.

**[ON SCREEN: The doubters' portraits are pushed to the edge of the frame by larger images of Woodward and the British Museum.]**

**NARRATOR:**
They were right. But they were outnumbered, and they were up against the British scientific establishment.

There was another problem: almost no one was allowed to examine the originals. Most researchers worked from plaster casts. The real bones stayed locked away in London.

**[ON SCREEN: A newspaper notice: "Charles Dawson, F.S.A., died August 10, 1916."]**

**NARRATOR:**
In August 1916, Charles Dawson died of an infection at the age of 52.

After his death, no more Piltdown fossils were ever found.

**[BEAT. Two seconds of silence.]**

## ACT FOUR: THE DAMAGE (5:30 – 6:45)

**[ON SCREEN: Archival footage of 1920s South Africa. A small fossil skull is cradled in a hand.]**

**NARRATOR:**
In 1924, an anatomist in South Africa named Raymond Dart studied a fossil skull taken from a limestone quarry at Taung. It was a child's skull, with a small, apelike brain but teeth that looked surprisingly human.

Dart called it *Australopithecus africanus* and argued that humanity began in Africa.

**[ON SCREEN: Dart's 1925 paper in *Nature*, with dismissive quotes fading in and out.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Much of the scientific establishment brushed it aside. Part of the reason was simple: the Taung child was the opposite of Piltdown. It had human teeth with an ape brain, while Piltdown had a human brain with an ape jaw.

If Piltdown was real, Taung had to be wrong.

**[ON SCREEN: A timeline graphic. A red line labelled "PILTDOWN" bends the path of human evolution away from Africa.]**

**NARRATOR:**
For decades, the fake helped push real discoveries to the sidelines and sent the study of human origins down the wrong path.

Meanwhile, Arthur Smith Woodward and his ally Arthur Keith were knighted. In 1938, a stone monument was unveiled at Barkham Manor to mark the spot where Piltdown Man was found.

It still stands there today.

## ACT FIVE: THE UNMASKING (6:45 – 8:30)

**[ON SCREEN: 1940s laboratory: glassware, white coats, a Bunsen burner.]**

**NARRATOR:**
The beginning of the end came from chemistry.

Buried bones slowly absorb fluorine from groundwater. The longer a bone sits in the ground, the more fluorine it contains. In 1949, a British Museum geologist named Kenneth Oakley applied this new fluorine test to Piltdown.

**[ON SCREEN: An animated bar chart. The animal fossils have tall fluorine bars. The Piltdown bones have tiny ones.]**

**NARRATOR:**
The animal fossils from the pit were genuinely ancient. The Piltdown bones were not. They were far younger than anyone had claimed.

**[ON SCREEN: Oxford in 1953. A portrait of Joseph Weiner.]**

**NARRATOR:**
In 1953, an Oxford anatomist named Joseph Weiner began to wonder about something no one had wanted to say out loud: what if none of it was real?

Together with Wilfrid Le Gros Clark and Kenneth Oakley, he took a hard look at the original bones.

**[ON SCREEN: Extreme macro close-ups of the molars. Animated arrows point to flat surfaces and scratch marks.]**

**NARRATOR:**
Under the microscope, the molars showed fine scratches and flat, unnaturally even surfaces. They had been filed down to look human.

The dark brown colour that made the bones look ancient was only on the surface. It had been applied with iron and chromic acid. The famous canine tooth had been painted brown.

**[ON SCREEN: The skull pieces separate from the jaw and drift apart.]**

**NARRATOR:**
The skull belonged to a modern human. The jaw came from an orangutan. Its joint with the skull had been broken off, so no one could see that the two pieces did not fit. The animal fossils had been brought in from elsewhere, and some were probably not even from Britain. The "cricket bat" had been carved with a steel knife.

**[ON SCREEN: A November 1953 newspaper headline: "PILTDOWN MAN FORGERY."]**

**NARRATOR:**
On the 21st of November 1953, the British Museum made it official. Piltdown Man was a fake.

Forty-one years after it was announced, the most famous ancestor in Britain had been exposed as a forgery.

## ACT SIX: WHODUNNIT? (8:30 – 9:30)

**[MUSIC: A playful, detective-style pizzicato.]**

**[ON SCREEN: A detective's corkboard. Suspects' photos are pinned up with red string.]**

**NARRATOR:**
That left one question. Who did it?

Over the years, almost everyone has been accused. Teilhard de Chardin was a suspect. So were Arthur Keith, the museum volunteer Martin Hinton and even Sir Arthur Conan Doyle, the creator of Sherlock Holmes, who lived nearby and played golf near the site.

**[ON SCREEN: The red string pulls tight towards Dawson's photo.]**

**NARRATOR:**
But the evidence keeps pointing back to one man.

Researchers have since identified dozens of other fakes in Charles Dawson's collection, including forged Roman bricks and doctored antiquities.

In 2016, a team led by Isabelle De Groote examined the Piltdown remains using DNA analysis and CT scans. The jaw and teeth appear to come from a single orangutan, and the same orangutan turns up at both Piltdown sites. Every piece had been prepared the same way, with the same staining and the same dental putty used to pack the holes.

That points to one hoaxer working alone.

And only one man was present at every find.

**[ON SCREEN: Text overlay: "The forger: almost certainly Charles Dawson."]**

**NARRATOR:**
So that is your answer. The man who "discovered" Piltdown almost certainly made it.

**[ON SCREEN: Final slow zoom into Dawson's portrait.]**

**[ON SCREEN: The Barkham Manor monument at dusk. Wind in the grass.]**

Piltdown Man is often remembered as science's greatest embarrassment.

But it is also a story about how science works. The forgery survived because people saw what they wanted to see. It fell because people kept asking questions, tested the evidence and were willing to admit they had been wrong.

The dawn man was never our ancestor.

But he remains one of history's most useful warnings.

## Production notes

- **Archival images:** The Natural History Museum (London) holds the Piltdown remains and many period photographs. John Cooke's 1915 painting *A Discussion of the Piltdown Skull* is ideal for the Act Two group shot. Check licensing before use.
