## Who we are talking to

- American adults aged 25 to 54. They are curious and they read history or watch true crime,
  but they are not specialists.
- They have heard "missing link" but probably not "Piltdown". Never assume prior knowledge of
  British institutions: say "London's Natural History Museum", not "the BM(NH)".
- They will stop watching the moment they feel lectured or padded.

## How the narrator talks

- **Register:** calm, low, investigative. Sounds like a detective laying out a case, not a
  teacher reading notes. The tension comes from facts in the right order, not from adjectives.
- **Sentences:** mostly short, written to be spoken in one breath. One long sentence is allowed
  per beat to build pressure, then a short one lands it.
- **Spelling and idiom:** American. "color", "gray", "skeptical"; dates as "December 18, 1912".
  British terms get a two-word gloss the first time ("a solicitor, a small-town lawyer").
- **Contractions:** used naturally ("it's", "didn't", "wasn't"), as people speak.
- **Questions:** used to open a loop, never as filler. Every question asked on camera is answered
  on camera, and the answer says it is the answer.
- **Wit:** dry, rare, and never at the expense of the facts.

## Words and moves the narrator never uses

- Generic AI and YouTube filler: "delve", "tapestry", "in a world where", "little did they know",
  "buckle up", "let's dive in", "it's worth noting", "the rest is history", "stay tuned",
  "shocking", "mind-blowing", "you won't believe".
- No channel intro, no "before we get started", no subscribe pitch before the payoff.
- No "some say" without saying who.
- No exclamation marks in narration.

## What the narrator will not claim

- No invented numbers, quotes, dates or sources. If a figure isn't verified, the line is written
  without it.
- Unproven guilt is never stated as fact. Use "the evidence points to" or "almost certainly",
  and say what the evidence is.
- Where sources disagree (dates, ages, cause of death), the narrator says less instead of picking
  one side silently.
- No claims about what the viewer personally was taught, believes or "would have believed".
  Address the viewer through what they can see ("You're looking at…"), not what they think.
- Never flatten a debate into "everyone believed". Name who accepted a claim and who doubted it.
- Every claim falls into one of three tiers, and the wording matches the tier:
  - **Fact:** stated plainly.
  - **Interpretation:** attributed ("the researchers concluded", "historians argue") or hedged
    ("probably", "almost certainly").
  - **Unresolved:** said to be open ("is still an open question"), or left out.
- Every script ships with a claims ledger (claim, tier, source) so the wording can be checked
  against the evidence.

## Pacing rules

- The first 30 seconds run about 60 to 75 words: hook, one promise, one visual open loop.
  There is no title sequence before the promise.
- Every revelation needs a setup earlier in the script, and when it pays off the narration says
  so ("Remember the line on the tooth?").
- A major reveal never lands as one more item in a list. Give it its own beat, its own picture
  and a moment of silence or music before the next fact.
- Hold back a "not yet": when a clue is only partial, the narrator says it isn't proof yet.
